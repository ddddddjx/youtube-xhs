I’ve reviewed thousands of CVs: recruiting at @BCG, my startup, & now hiring for @OpenAI. So this applies across consulting / professional services, tech, & other industries.

**3.** (ID: 2117600611122233386 - second link)
Before the tips, a couple of points:

1. Get a warm referral if you can. But if you don’t know anyone, you have to apply online - that’s how I got into OpenAI. I was given a chance and the rest is history.

2. CVs are a horribly reductionist way of describing yourself; they cannot capture who you are as a person. But they can demonstrate that you have relevant experience & are able to communicate that well. The goal is to show it’s worth having a live chat.

Tips:

**4.**
1. Keep it concise: ideally one page, but relevance matters more than an arbitrary limit. Show me you’re an excellent written communicator & can prioritise.

**5.**
2. No irrelevant experiences: make the relevance clear & strip out anything not adding signal. I’m proud of my high school achievements, but they’re usually not relevant now!

**6.**
3. Show impact, not action: quantify your impact, don’t just list actions. “Re-architected an agent” doesn’t mean it was a good use of time; but if you reduced latency by 2s & that improved customer satisfaction, you’ve made impact. If you’re a seller, talk revenue.

**7.**
4. Make it easy to skim: bold the important stuff and put your strongest evidence first under each experience. In a mountain of CVs, make it easy to quickly see why yours deserves an interview.

**8.**
As in my previous posts: clear communication, relevance, and evidence of impact matter throughout the application process.

**9.**
I want to spot great candidates; make it easy for me to see what you’d bring to the team & why we should chat.

More tips, & some pretty cool new roles (!!), coming soon  🤝

**2.**
Too many great candidates sell themselves short in interviews. Often, it’s the simple things.

The situation: 500,000 people saw my open role post; a few thousand applied; of those, you’ve made it to interview. That’s an achievement in itself.

So here are my top tips to nail the behavioural interviews. (More technical tips coming in future posts!)

**3.**
1. Perfect your intro: it's the only part you *know* is coming, so prepare it. Keep it short & tailored to the role. The most common failures I see are talking for far too long, and listing experiences without explaining relevance. See my previous post on cold outreach; the same principles apply.

**4.**
2. Structured, clear comms: I’m looking for unicorns that can go very deep technically, but also explain the big picture. Start with a clear, structured answer; then let me dig into the details. “I’ll start at a high level, but happy to go deeper”. Going too detailed too soon is a common failure for highly technical people.

**5.**
3. It's a conversation, not a recital: prepare examples, but listen to the question you’re actually being asked. I want to understand how you think, not listen to a script. If you’re telling a story, link it back to the question & the role.

**6.**
4. Show excitement: do your research on the interviewer & the role. Some candidates refer to my essays: sometimes this is flattery (to which I am not immune...), but the best candidates say what they *changed* because of it. It's not “I liked your essay”; it’s “I’m learning all the time, and I use that to create real impact; plus I’m organised enough to have researched you”.

**7.**
Turn up prepared, but the aim is to discover whether it’s a mutual fit - so a two way conversation, with clear communication, is the best way to do that.

I genuinely want you to show your best self, so I don’t miss amazing candidates!
