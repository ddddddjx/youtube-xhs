In fourth grade, my teacher handed my parents a recommendation for secondary school. My grades were mediocre, and I had a tendency to challenge authority. So she recommended a school that wouldn’t allow me to pursue university later on.

Her message was clear: I wasn’t good enough for the best schools.

Luckily, in Germany, parents have the final say. Mine ignored her recommendation and sent me to a great school anyway. But things didn’t exactly improve. During my first year, I was on the verge of being kicked out. I was struggling in all the main subjects. The only thing I was really good at was sports.

One day, my dad got a call from my teacher. He came into my room and told me I had 3 months to turn things around. Otherwise, I’d have to leave the school.

I was 10 years old and didn’t fully understand the gravity of the situation. I didn’t particularly care about being a good student. I just wanted to have fun. But I certainly didn’t want to leave the school.

So I started putting in the effort. Doing my homework, participating in class, studying for tests. Nothing extraordinary, just the basics. But I did them consistently, every single day.

Long story short, I made it. A few years later, my teacher said publicly she had never seen such a transformation in her entire career. I had gone from nearly being kicked out to becoming one of the best students in my year.

I learned that you can achieve things that once seemed highly unlikely if you truly commit to them.

Later on, when I entered the startup world, this belief evolved into something more extreme: you can achieve anything if you just work hard enough.

Hard work. Blood, sweat and tears. That was the way to succeed and I embraced much of that mindset. I remember going to the founder of a startup I worked for and recommending that he fire 50% of the engineers simply because they left the office before 6pm. I even suggested cutting everyone’s salary by 20% to see who would stay, believing it would help identify those who were truly committed. At one point, the co-founder even asked me to stop replying to client messages at 1 AM because she didn’t want clients to think we were working like slaves. Ironically, the company failed despite operating in a growing market and having founders and employees who worked incredibly hard.

At 28, I became one of Europe’s youngest VC partners. We got lucky and built a fund that ended up ranking among the top 1% in Europe.

Now, as I’m building something of my own, I find myself reflecting on how I work and how the people I admire most approach their craft. I realised: My best work rarely happened when I was pushing the hardest. It happened when I felt aligned, and the work didn’t really feel like work, but rather seemed almost effortless.

When we observe the best people at their craft, it often seems effortless. There is fun, there is play, there is lightheartedness. There is flow.

Perhaps no one embodies this better than tennis prodigy Carlos Alcaraz.

But it wasn’t always this way. In 2024, Alcaraz struggled with the relentless demands of professional tennis. Too many tournaments and obligations had gradually drained the joy from the sport he once loved.

“My fear is that tennis becomes an obligation. I constantly feel that I am trapped. And that triggers in me anxiety, frustration and doubt. So, I ask myself: Do I want to live this life?”

In 2023, Alcaraz clashed with his then-coach Juan Carlos Ferrero. Ferrero believed that “you have to be a slave to the game” and argued that “Djokovic sacrificed more than Alcaraz.” Alcaraz, however, had other plans. He wanted to go to Ibiza and party, just 3 weeks before Wimbledon.

“They all knew what I was going there for. I wanted to get wasted. I made the most of it. When I got back, I won Queens and Wimbledon.”

He even upped it in 2024, spending 6 days partying in Ibiza instead of 4, just 2 weeks before Wimbledon. He lost at Queen’s but then went on to win Wimbledon.

„I am someone who thinks, if it works for you, you have to keep doing it“

Interestingly, Alcaraz and Ferrero eventually parted ways. By the age of 23 (Alcaraz’s current age), Djokovic had won 1 Grand Slam and had never been world No. 1. Alcaraz, meanwhile, has already won 7 Grand Slams and spent over 66 weeks as world No. 1.

Alcaraz once asked Roger Federer how he managed to stay motivated for so many years. Federer replied:

”You have to enjoy the journey. Always make sure you’re having fun, no matter where you are. Take a break from time to time, get away from it all, and when you come back, you’ll feel excited again.”

Federer himself revealed that for years, he trained just 2 hours a day and made breakfast for his family most mornings. He described how much joy his wife and kids brought into the house. At night, he would put his head on the pillow and think, “Man, this was a great day.” No other player has been described as “effortless” as often as Federer.

Steve Kerr, former head coach of the US national basketball team, Olympic gold medal winner and winner of 9 NBA titles, commented later that Federer is a great example of how long-term success isn’t about outworking everybody else.

You can truly see this philosophy in how Alcaraz carries himself. He dances before matches, laughs after points and jokes around with his team afterwards. His game looks loose, easy, almost as if he’s just playing. Because he is.

At the end of October 2026, just one day before winning another ATP tournament, Alcaraz said:

“Now, I am just myself all the time”.

You don’t just see this phenomenon in sports. You can observe it in pretty much every discipline.

She is probably the most successful female artist in recent years, has won a Grammy and has 53 million monthly listeners on Spotify alone. A few of her quotes offer a glimpse into how she approaches work:

“I only wanna work with nice people. If it feels kind of icky in the studio, I just walk out and say I feel sick.”

“I am such a present person. It’s like my past doesn’t exist. Everyday is a new day.”

“I don’t write everyday. My best songs are written if I have a 2h nice chat with someone and then I write the song itself in 1h.”

“I was thinking a lot about my comfort zone. And you know how people are, like, you should push yourself out of your comfort zone? I tried that. I didn’t like it. Why can’t I be comfortable? Why do I have to be in distress to make good music? Why can’t I just be happy? So, I tried to create the best environment for myself to work in, and it went great.”

If you look more closely at what experts in the field and scientific studies say, this seemingly counterintuitive picture starts to make sense.

When we act in alignment with ourselves, we feel at peace. We feel content.

A 2019 Oxford study found that a 1-point increase in happiness on a scale from 0 to 10 corresponded to 12% higher productivity. In more complex tasks, happier workers were even 20% more productive. Other studies suggest that lower stress levels support better judgment, reduce impulsive decisions, enable more creative thinking and improve resilience.

Personal Reflections

You might be surprised, but I believe in hard work. Blood, sweat and tears are inevitable if you want to build something truly great. There has to be a push for 10% of the time. 

However, the other 90% have to be a natural pull. Play, lightheartedness and fun are crucial. This has to be the default.

Will there be people who won’t achieve the outcomes they desire because they aren’t willing to put in those 10%? Absolutely. And it’s sad because they will most likely feel regret at the end of their lives, realising they never lived up to their potential.

However, are there many people out there who will never achieve their desired outcomes, while feeling miserable because they spend 90% of their time pushing? Yes. And that is even sadder.

So, what’s the bottom line here?

Doing you is the ultimate advantage. Leaning into your own way of doing things.

Hard work is not a moat. Every ambitious person I know works “hard”. Uniqueness is a moat. Self-alignment, I believe, is not just the key to professional “success” but ultimately to contentment, happiness and fulfillment.

If you enjoy the game, and the way you are playing it is very you, why ever stop playing it?

To say it with Chris Martin and Warren Buffett: If you can make things weirdly you, you might just find yourself tap-dancing to work, even at 96.
